One click, against the alternatives

Most astrophotography software stops at the stack, or hands you a box of tools to finish the image yourself. OriginStack is built for the other way round: point it at a night of Celestron Origin frames, press Start, and get a finished, colour-calibrated, noise-reduced, stretched image, with the linear stack kept for anyone who wants to go further. This page sets that against the tools Origin owners usually try, with the measurements behind every number.

What the one click does after the stack

Stacking is where most tools stop. In OriginStack it is the halfway point: the finishing chain below runs in the same click, in this order, with settings chosen for the kind of target it recognised. Each step can be switched off, and every one is described in how it works.

Colour calibrated against Gaiaon by default

Before finishing starts, the stack's colours are set from the stars in the field and their catalogued Gaia colours, so a Sun-like star comes out white and the rest follow. No reference image or manual white balance.

Hot pixels and star maskon by default

Leftover hot pixels are cleaned per colour channel, and the stars are found once and reused, so every later step knows what is a star and what is sky or nebula.

Gradients removedon by default

Light pollution, moonlight and vignetting leave a slope of brightness across the frame. A background model is fitted to the sky between the stars and the object, each colour against its own sky, and subtracted, with the object protected.

Colour noise and a neutral skyon by default

Coloured speckle in the dark sky is smoothed without touching the brightness detail, and the sky is set to a neutral, even black level instead of a tinted one.

Noise reductionon by default

A wavelet denoiser that protects elongated structure such as filaments and spiral arms, chosen after benchmarking the alternatives on scenes with known truth.

Learned deconvolutionautomatic for Origin sessions

A small neural network trained on real Origin sessions, against sharper survey images of the same sky, undoes the night's seeing blur and removes noise in one step. On held-out targets it gave the narrowest stars of every option, with no dark rings and no invented detail, checked against independent stacks.

Star reduction and local contraston by default

Stars are narrowed without losing their colour or peak, so they stop crowding out the object, and mid-scale detail on the object is lifted against the noise at each scale.

Edges and the final skyon by default

The ragged border left by alignment is cleaned up, and the sky is flattened and neutralised one last time after everything else.

The stretchon by default

A generalised hyperbolic stretch brings up faint light while keeping bright cores from blowing out, and an extended bright region is rolled off rather than clipped. You get the JPEG, and can pick another of ten stretches afterwards without running again.

Measured, not claimed

The same frames and the same bias, dark and flat for every tool, on one machine, each at default-style settings. Star width is fitted on the same stars in each pair of stacks, each on its own pixel grid. The full method, and the times these numbers were corrected, are on the home page.

Sharper than all three

OriginStack's stars are 1 to 12% narrower than Siril's on every session measured, narrower than DeepSkyStacker's, and 4 to 27% narrower than the telescope's own stack.

No noise penalty

A sharper stack is noisier per pixel, so the fair test blurs it to the other tool's sharpness first. Then OriginStack is quieter than Siril in every channel; at the scale of a star it ties the Origin's own stack.

Every frame used

OriginStack used all 114, 158 and 532 frames of the three Siril sessions, where Siril kept 111, 157 and 525.

A handful of sessions from one camera are a small sample, and the other tools have many settings that were left at their defaults. tools/bench_vs_siril.py repeats the comparison on your own frames and scores a stack from any other program with --external; tools/bench_vs_origin.py does the same against the Origin's own stack.

Fast, and the finished image included

The Omega Nebula, 114 frames, on an 8-core laptop: 62 seconds from the raw files to the finished image. To the stack alone, OriginStack is faster than Siril on all three sessions measured, even though it also scores, white-balances and finds stars in every frame.

Over 80 kernels in Rustbuilt in

The hot paths are compiled native code: calibration, debayering, hot-pixel repair, alignment warps, the stacking combines, background fitting, star detection, denoising and photometry. Each has a pure-Python fallback, and most return exactly the same numbers as the code they replaced.

Measured against the NumPy and SciPy code they replaced: the default sigma-clip stack about 37 times faster, the fused weighted combine about 100 times, hot-pixel repair 25 to 55 times, the background sample fit about 31 times, the rotated-frame warp about 5 times, batch photometry about 150 times.

Built around your CPUautomatic

Frame processing is limited by memory speed, not arithmetic, so OriginStack runs single-threaded workers on three quarters of the physical cores, which beat the obvious all-threads layout by 7 to 10%. The release builds run on any 64-bit PC; where a kernel has a faster AVX2 version, it is chosen when the program starts.

Big nights in small memorystreaming

Frames are processed one at a time and the aligned frames kept in memory only when there is room, with a fifth of it always left free; otherwise they go to temporary files read in bands. 500 frames need about the same memory as 50.

Many nights, finished oncemulti-session

Several sessions of one target are stacked separately where the sky rotated too far to pool them, registered onto the deepest, and finished once on the combined stack.

Seven Orion Nebula sessions, 456 frames: 8 minutes 16 seconds, from 15 minutes 12 seconds before this was reworked.

Rerun the finish in seconds--from-stack

Every run saves its linear stack and settings, so trying another stretch or finishing setting reruns only the finish, with the slow early steps cached.

Which one to use

OriginStack

You have an Origin, or any colour camera, and want the best finished image from a night with one click, on your own computer, for free. The linear stack is kept if you later want to finish it elsewhere.

PixInsight

You want complete manual control of every processing step and are happy to learn a large toolset and pay for it. OriginStack's linear FITS is a good starting point for it.

Siril

You want a free, scriptable toolkit and enjoy finishing the image yourself, step by step.

The Origin app

You want to see the image build up at the telescope, live. For the final picture, OriginStack's stars measured 4 to 27% sharper from the same frames. Ten sessions side by side show the difference.
